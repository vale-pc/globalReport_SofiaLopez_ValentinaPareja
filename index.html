Is There a Significant Gender Wage Gap?
A Statistical Analysis Using Hypothesis Testing

Global Economics · Grade 11 · Term 3 Learning Evidence 4 · 2025–2026
Teacher: Nicolás López Cuéllar

Introduction

Income inequality has emerged as one of the most pressing economic challenges of the twenty-first century. According to the United States Census Bureau (2024), real median household income was $80,610 in 2023, representing a 4.0 percent increase from 2022 — the first statistically significant annual increase since 2019. Despite such aggregate gains, the distribution of those gains remains deeply unequal. Data from the Economic Policy Institute (as cited in the Institute for Policy Studies, 2024) indicates that between 1979 and 2024, average hourly compensation grew by only 29.4 percent in real terms while worker productivity rose 80.9 percent, illustrating a persistent and widening productivity–pay gap.

At the global level, the International Labour Organization (2024) reports that real wage growth in advanced economies was revised downward to −2.8 percent on average, while emerging economies saw modest gains of approximately 1.8 percent, underscoring the asymmetrical nature of wage growth across income groups.

This report uses a two-sample z-test to investigate whether a statistically significant difference in mean annual income exists between Female and Male workers, employing the interactive hypothesis-testing webpage developed for this assignment.

Research Question

Is there a statistically significant difference in mean annual income between Female and Male workers in a sample dataset, at the 5% significance level?

Methodology

A two-sample z-test for the difference in means was conducted using the CSV upload feature of the interactive webpage. The uploaded CSV file (gender_wage_gap.csv) contained two groups — Female (benchmark) and Male (test group) — with a numeric annual income variable in USD. The webpage automatically computed the summary statistics for each group, calculated the test statistic, and produced a normal-curve visualization of the p-value region.

The following hypotheses were established:

H₀: μ_Female = μ_Male  (No significant difference in mean income between Female and Male groups)
H₁: μ_Female ≠ μ_Male  (Significant difference in mean income between Female and Male groups)

The test was configured as a two-tailed test at significance level α = 0.05, corresponding to critical values of z = ±1.96.

CSV Data Upload Procedure

To use the C9 CSV mode, the user navigates to the C9 — CSV Mode tab and clicks the upload zone (or drags and drops a CSV file). The page reads the file, identifies all available columns, and presents dropdown menus for selecting the group column, the value column, the benchmark group, and the test group. After choosing the test direction and significance level, clicking ▶ Run Hypothesis Test computes all statistics automatically and renders the normal curve with the shaded p-value region.

The dataset used for this report is a two-group gender wage sample (gender_wage_gap.csv), containing 20 observations per group (Female and Male) based on real U.S. annual income figures consistent with the U.S. Census Bureau (2024) data. The file was uploaded to the C9 mode, the group column was set to gender, the value column to annual_income_USD, the benchmark group to Female, and the test group to Male. Statistical Decision

The p-value (1.332 × 10⁻¹⁵) is far below α = 0.05. Therefore, we reject H₀. There is overwhelming statistical evidence to conclude that the mean annual income of Males ($65,045) is significantly different from — and higher than — that of Females ($52,310) in this sample. The test statistic z = 8.006 falls well beyond the critical region of ±1.96, confirming the rejection.

Discussion

The result (z = 8.006, p ≈ 0.000) provides overwhelming statistical evidence of a significant income gap between Female and Male groups. The mean annual income of Males ($65,045) exceeded that of Females ($52,310) by approximately $12,735, a difference confirmed as statistically significant at the 5% level. This finding is consistent with documented evidence of persistent wage inequality. The U.S. Census Bureau (2024) reports that while median household income increased in 2023, income disparities across demographic groups remain substantial. Globally, the ILO (2024) found that wage gains are unevenly distributed, with many workers — especially women — lagging behind in real purchasing power. The Institute for Policy Studies (2024) further documents that compensation growth has not kept pace with productivity gains, and these gaps are especially pronounced along gender lines.

Type I Error (α = 0.05): There is a 5% probability of concluding that a significant income difference exists when in reality none does. However, given the extremely low p-value observed here (1.332 × 10⁻¹⁵), a Type I error is exceedingly unlikely.

Type II Error (β): There is some probability of failing to detect a real income difference. Given the large effect size (z = 8.006), this error is also very unlikely in this dataset. In broader policy contexts, however, Type II errors could cause policymakers to overlook genuine gender-based wage inequality, delaying necessary corrective interventions.

Conclusion

This report applied a two-tailed z-test to investigate whether Female and Male workers differ significantly in mean annual income. The result (z = 8.006, p ≈ 0.000) led to rejection of H₀, confirming a statistically significant gender wage gap. The interactive webpage enabled efficient computation and visualisation of the result. Interpreted alongside real-world data from the U.S. Census Bureau (2024), the ILO (2024), and the Institute for Policy Studies (2024), the findings demonstrate that the gender wage gap is not merely anecdotal — it is statistically substantiated. These results reinforce the importance of evidence-based economic policy, particularly for addressing systemic inequalities that persist despite aggregate income growth.

README — How to Use the C9 CSV Mode

Opening the webpage: Open index.html in any modern browser, or visit the GitHub Pages deployment link.

Testing C9 mode with the three provided CSV files:
1. Click the C9 — CSV Mode tab.
2. Click the upload zone (or drag & drop) to select one of the three test CSV files (test_database_1.csv, test_database_2.csv, test_database_3.csv).
3. Use the dropdown menus to set the Group Column, Value Column, Benchmark Group, and Test Group.
4. Choose the test direction and significance level α.
5. Click ▶ Run Hypothesis Test.
6. The page displays: summary statistics table · normal curve with shaded p-value · test statistic · p-value · critical value · decision · conclusion.

Expected CSV structure:
Two columns minimum — one with group labels (e.g., group) and one with numeric values or binary 0/1 outcomes (e.g., income or success).
