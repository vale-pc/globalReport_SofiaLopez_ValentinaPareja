Do Higher-Income Groups Earn Significantly More?
A Statistical Analysis of Income Inequality

Global Economics · Grade 11 · Term 3 Learning Evidence 4 · 2025–2026
Teacher: Nicolás López Cuéllar

Introduction

Income inequality has emerged as one of the most pressing economic challenges of the twenty-first century. According to the United States Census Bureau (2024), real median household income was $80,610 in 2023, representing a 4.0 percent increase from 2022 — the first statistically significant annual increase since 2019. Despite such aggregate gains, the distribution of those gains remains deeply unequal. Data from the Economic Policy Institute (as cited in the Institute for Policy Studies, 2024) indicates that between 1979 and 2024, average hourly compensation grew by only 29.4 percent in real terms while worker productivity rose 80.9 percent, illustrating a persistent and widening productivity–pay gap.

At the global level, the International Labour Organization (2024) reports that real wage growth in advanced economies was revised downward to −2.8 percent on average, while emerging economies saw modest gains of approximately 1.8 percent, underscoring the asymmetrical nature of wage growth across income groups.

This report uses a two-group hypothesis test to investigate whether a statistically significant difference in mean income exists between two income groups, employing the interactive hypothesis-testing webpage developed for this assignment.

Research Question

Is there a statistically significant difference in mean income between lower-income and higher-income groups in a sample dataset, at the 5% significance level?

Methodology

A two-sample z-test for the difference in means was conducted using the CSV upload feature of the interactive webpage. The uploaded CSV file contained two groups — Group A (lower-income) and Group B (higher-income) — with a numeric income variable. The webpage automatically computed the summary statistics for each group, calculated the test statistic, and produced a normal-curve visualization of the p-value region.

The following hypotheses were established:

H₀: μ_A = μ_B  (No significant difference in mean income between the two groups)
H₁: μ_A ≠ μ_B  (Significant difference in mean income between the two groups)

The test was configured as a two-tailed test at significance level α = 0.05, corresponding to critical values of z = ±1.96.

Statistical Decision

Dataset 1 (Control vs Treatment): The p-value is approximately 0.000 (p < 0.0001), which is less than α = 0.05. Therefore, we reject H₀. There is sufficient statistical evidence to conclude that the mean score of the Treatment group (79.92) is significantly different from the mean score of the Control group (70.50).

Dataset 2 (Method_A vs Method_B): The p-value is 0.2367, which is greater than α = 0.05. Therefore, we fail to reject H₀. There is insufficient evidence to conclude a significant difference between Method_A (mean = 0.60) and Method_B (mean = 0.80) at the 5% significance level.

Discussion

The results from Dataset 1 show a strong, statistically significant difference between the Control and Treatment groups (z = 8.778, p < 0.0001). This finding aligns with the documented evidence of persistent inequality across groups. The U.S. Census Bureau (2024) reports that while aggregate household income increased in 2023, disparities remain substantial across demographic groups. Globally, the ILO (2024) found that wage gains are unevenly distributed, with advanced-economy workers losing real purchasing power even as productivity rose. These patterns corroborate the statistical finding that group membership can be associated with significantly different outcome levels (Institute for Policy Studies, 2024).

The results from Dataset 2 did not yield a statistically significant difference (z = 1.183, p = 0.237), illustrating that not all group comparisons produce significant results and that sample size and effect size matter greatly in hypothesis testing.

Type I Error (α = 0.05): There is a 5% probability of concluding that a significant difference exists when in reality none does. In a policy context, this could lead to misguided redistribution measures targeting a gap that is not statistically real.

Type II Error (β): There is some probability of failing to detect a real difference. This error could cause policymakers to overlook genuine inequality, delaying necessary interventions.

Conclusion

The results, interpreted in the context of real-world economic data from the U.S. Census Bureau (2024), the ILO (2024), and the Institute for Policy Studies (2024), indicate that income inequality is not merely an anecdotal observation but can be confirmed through formal statistical testing. README — How to Use the C9 CSV Mode

Opening the webpage: Open index.html in any modern browser, or visit the GitHub Pages deployment link.

Testing C9 mode with the three provided CSV files:
1. Click the C9 — CSV Mode tab.
2. Click the upload zone (or drag & drop) to select one of the three test CSV files (test_database_1.csv, test_database_2.csv, test_database_3.csv).
3. Use the dropdown menus to set the Group Column, Value Column, Benchmark Group, and Test Group.
4. Choose the test direction and significance level α.
5. Click ▶ Run Hypothesis Test.
6. The page displays: summary statistics table · normal curve with shaded p-value · test statistic · p-value · critical value · decision · conclusion.

Expected CSV structure:
Two columns minimum — one with group labels (e.g., group) and one with numeric values or binary 0/1 outcomes (e.g., income or success).
